The measurement problems that make published safety comparisons difficult to evaluate, and what the independent studies find. Why safety records cannot be compared

Waymo's own comparative safety claim circulates in at least three forms across 2026 sources: 13 times fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes than human drivers (June 2026), 90 percent fewer serious crashes (February 2026), and 91 percent fewer serious injury crashes (December 2025). These are not necessarily contradictory, since they may cover different denominators and periods. Published without their methodology, though, they are difficult to compare or verify. I show all three with dates, and note that the underlying exposure data is not independently auditable.

Mileage figures disagree the same way: 170M-plus autonomous miles per a June 2026 Waymo statement, against 220M-plus rider-only miles through March 2026 in earlier compilations. Rider-only miles and total autonomous miles are different denominators, and a comparison that does not define its denominator first cannot be evaluated. The general rule this page applies: define terms, date figures, and prefer filings over press.

What the independent studies find, and where they disagree

Everything above is drawn from the operators. Three third-party studies now exist, and the useful thing about them is that they do not agree — because they do not pick the same benchmark.

The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, in July 2026, compared roughly 50 million Waymo miles driven in San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles and Austin between 2021 and 2024 against 222 billion human-driven miles over the same period. It found a 68 percent lower police-reportable crash rate and 81 percent fewer injury crashes per mile.

Swiss Re, in December 2024, took a different route and counted insurance claims across 25.3 million rider-only miles: 88 percent fewer property-damage claims and 92 percent fewer bodily-injury claims than its own human baseline drawn from more than 200 billion miles of exposure. Nine property claims and two injury claims, against 78 and 26 expected. That work is peer-reviewed, published by Kusano and colleagues in Traffic Injury Prevention.

Then the Sam Schwartz Transportation Research Program at Hunter College, with Open Plans, in July 2026, asked a sharper question: not whether robotaxis beat the average American motorist, but whether they beat the driver they actually replace. Measured against New York City's professional for-hire drivers — who are already around 14 times safer than the average US motorist — Waymo's rate of crashes causing serious injury or death came out 1.5 times higher per million miles, while its overall injury-crash rate came out 67 percent lower. Both of those are in the same study.

What that actually means

The disagreement is not a flaw in the research; it is the denominator problem from the section above, showing up in real work. Against all drivers everywhere, the safety case is strong and independently corroborated. Against a professional driver in a dense city, on the most serious outcomes, it is not yet established.

Nor is the underlying data good enough to settle it. IIHS had to discard about a quarter of the reported automated-vehicle crashes as duplicates, off public roads, or below any reporting threshold, and judged only 22 percent of 736 on-road crashes with automation engaged to be likely police-reportable at all. The human baseline is shakier still: roughly half of all crashes and about a third of injuries are never reported to police. IIHS said plainly that current collection is not good enough to monitor autonomous-vehicle safety on an ongoing basis, and the Hunter College authors cautioned that limited miles and data constraints weigh against firm conclusions in either direction. On the evidence available in mid-2026, anyone claiming this is settled — in either direction — is ahead of the data.

When one fault stops an entire fleet at once

The question the industry has not answered

The Wuhan outage and the San Francisco power cut are qualitatively different from every per-vehicle defect above, because they were simultaneous and fleet-wide, and because each turned a private product into a public-safety problem within minutes. Industry engineers attributed the Wuhan stall to a safety self-check mechanism, which is the same shape as Waymo's dark-signal behaviour: each vehicle failed safe, and the fleet failed the city. Individually correct, collectively catastrophic. Robotaxis are now an emergency-services dependency, and no operator has published what its fleet does when the cloud, the grid or the network goes away.

The numbers nobody publishes

Comparable per-mile exposure data across operators. Every safety claim on this page is self-reported against a denominator its author chose. Until operators publish audited exposure data on a common definition, the honest summary of AV safety is: the per-vehicle record is encouraging, the fleet-level record contains two significant failures, and the comparison to human driving currently rests on figures the operators compile themselves.
