A robotaxi ride is a loop,
and the loop is an industry.

Every driverless ride runs the same circuit: a request, a driver made of software, a vehicle built around it, and a pitlane that turns the car straight back out. Around that loop sit 561 organisations. This site maps all of them.

■ IN THE SIMPLEST POSSIBLE TERMS

What is actually driving the car?

An autonomous vehicle is a car that drives itself from one point to the next without a human operating it. Most people will meet one the way they meet a normal taxi: through an app, at a kerb.

Three technologies make it work. Hardware lets the vehicle see, using cameras and, in most designs, lidar and radar. Software decides what the vehicle should do next, trained on millions of real and simulated miles. And the vehicle itself is engineered so that its steering, throttle and brakes can be moved by that software, with backups for each, because there is no longer a person to grab the wheel.

That is the essential mechanism, described in a paragraph. Beneath it sits an industry of 561 organisations across eleven layers, from the companies that etch the chips to the regulators who sign the permits. The rest of this site is that industry, drawn to scale.

■ WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES

Same kerb, different machine

A robotaxi is priced like a taxi and hailed like a taxi. Operationally, it differs from human-driven ride-hail across most of the dimensions that carry cost.

Human-driven rideshareAutonomous rideshare
Who is liable in a crash The driver, through personal or commercial insurance, with the platform mostly arm's length. The operator and increasingly the manufacturer, under a legal regime still being written. The UK's 2024 Act moves liability to the authorised self-driving entity.
What happens when it rains hard The driver adjusts speed and continues, using their own judgement. The operating domain may pause service. Waymo recalled about 3,800 vehicles in May 2026 for driving onto flooded roads, then imposed weather limits.
Who cleans the car The driver, between shifts, judged by star rating. Depot crews on a schedule: Transdev runs Waymo depots in five metros, and startups now sell robotic pitstops that inspect, clean and charge in a parking space.
The marginal cost of one ride Mostly the driver's time, roughly 70 to 80 percent of the fare. Mostly amortised hardware, energy, insurance and remote operations. Third-party estimates put Waymo near $1.98 per mile in 2025; no operator publishes the number.
Who you complain to The driver first, then support. An app, an SOS button and a remote operations team. Wuhan's outage in March 2026 showed what happens when that channel fails with passengers inside.
What a power cut does Drivers treat dark signals as four-way stops and traffic keeps moving. San Francisco, December 2025: robotaxis halted at dark signals and gridlocked streets. Fail-safes behave correctly per vehicle and badly per fleet.
Who decides where it can go The driver, within the law. A geofence agreed with regulators, city by city. Tesla's Austin map covers the whole metro while its unsupervised fleet there is about 17 cars (July 2026).
What it does between rides The driver waits where experience says demand will be. Fleet software repositions constantly; industry estimates put empty miles as high as 44 percent, which is why depot placement is a science.
