Unit economics: break the business yourself, operator by operator

Every operator on this map runs the same machine: a vehicle costing $50,000 to $150,000 with its autonomy kit, driven as many paid miles a day as demand allows, against costs that are mostly fixed per mile. The lever is utilisation — a private car works an hour a day, a robotaxi can work twenty — and the one number that matters is fully-loaded cost per revenue mile. Third-party analysis puts Waymo's 2025 figure near $1.98; every bull case in the sector is the claim that it halves. Published component breakdowns land at $0.30-0.50 per mile for a mature operation — a destination nobody operates at today, and the gap between it and $1.98 is the industry's entire remaining homework.

Provenance warning, before you quote anything. No operator publishes fully-loaded cost per mile. The $1.98 figure is a third-party analyst estimate circulating across several publications, and at least one trail leads back to a Reddit analysis. That is the state of public knowledge in this industry, which is exactly why every assumption in the table below is editable: substitute your own and stop depending on anyone's estimate, including mine. What each operator believes, and what it costs

The tables above carry the detail; the philosophies behind them are simple to state, and almost every strategic difference in the sector is downstream of one choice: what the car is allowed to know.

Cameras only. Tesla bets that a camera-only stack on a mass-produced consumer vehicle, with no lidar bill and no mapping bill, halves the cost line — an $8 five-mile ride against Waymo's $15-17. The wager is that software eventually closes the gap that redundant sensors close today; its Austin base fare tripling in March 2026 suggests the first data argued back.

Sensor fusion. Waymo and Zoox bet the other way: lidar, radar and cameras cross-checking each other, high-definition maps underneath, and a kit cost that must be engineered down rather than deleted. Waymo's sub-$20,000 sixth-generation kit is that engineering in public, carried by a $126B valuation on roughly $355M of revenue while scale catches up. The wager is that safety cases, regulators and insurers price redundancy long before they price promises.

Cheaper vehicles, denser cities. Baidu and Pony.ai take sensor fusion but attack the other term of the equation: a $28,000 platform at Chinese fare levels in cities dense enough to keep utilisation high. Their breakeven claims are vehicle-level in single cities, not company profitability.

Freight, not fares. Aurora bets on trucking, where the benchmark is a real freight rate rather than a subsidised fare — honest revenue, thinner headroom than the robotaxi story suggests.

Where the arithmetic actually stands

Uber's CFO said plainly, on the record, in August 2025: autonomous vehicles today are not profitable. Alphabet's Other Bets, which contains Waymo, lost $2.1B in Q1 2026. Every published breakeven claim in the industry is per-vehicle in a single city. The bull case requires the cost line to halve, and the evidence that it will is a hardware roadmap, not an income statement. That is not a dismissal; it is a timestamp.
