■ THE PERMISSION

Who is allowed to drive,
and who decides

In June 2026 the world's road-vehicle regulators agreed, for the first time, on what a driverless vehicle must prove. This area moves quickly, so every item below carries a date, and the fastest-changing sections are marked.

The first international framework for driverless systems

UNECE's World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations, WP.29, adopted the first harmonised international framework for fully driverless automated driving systems at its 23 to 26 June 2026 session, after the GRVA working party approved the draft that January. It arrives as two parallel instruments: a UN Regulation under the 1958 Agreement for type-approval markets such as the EU, Japan, Korea and the UK, and a Global Technical Regulation under the 1998 Agreement for self-certification markets including the US, Canada and China. Roughly ninety existing UN vehicle regulations were amended alongside so that current rules still apply to vehicles without steering wheels or pedals.

The core requirement is goal-based rather than prescriptive: an automated driving system must be free from unreasonable safety risk and perform at least at the level of a competent and careful human driver, evidenced through a safety management system, a structured safety case, credible testing including virtual toolchains, and continuous in-service monitoring. It entered force about a month after adoption. It authorises nothing by itself; it gives every contracting state a common basis for national approvals, which is why it matters structurally. Three weeks earlier the IMO adopted the maritime equivalent, covered on Beyond Roads: road and sea converged on the same philosophy, goal-based safety cases with in-service monitoring, within a month, after a decade of working separately.

Six regimes, five questions each

Who decides
NHTSA governs vehicle safety standards, exemptions, defects and research; states govern licensing, traffic law, registration and deployment permission. There is no federal AV safety statute: the SELF DRIVE Act of 2026 remains a bipartisan draft, after 2017 and 2021 versions died, and a separate February 2026 bill proposes limiting city and state authority over AVs. Deployment therefore happens state by state and city by city.
The current instrument
The AV Framework announced 24 April 2025 on three principles: prioritise safety of ongoing operations, remove unnecessary barriers, enable commercial deployment. Part 555 exemptions were streamlined from 13 June 2025, capped at 2,500 vehicles per manufacturer per year; Part 591, historically for imports, was opened to US-built vehicles in April 2025. The Standing General Order keeps monthly crash reporting in force.
What is permitted today
Fully driverless paid passenger service in permissive states: Arizona, Texas, Georgia, Florida, Nevada and more, with California running its own two-regulator permit ladder. Texas deserves the callout: its AV law took effect 1 September 2025, and TxDMV authorisation before commercial driverless operation became enforceable 28 May 2026, governing the state where Waymo, Tesla and Avride all operate.
What changes next
FMVSS modernisation on four tracks: FMVSS 102 and 103/104 NPRMs issued 16 March 2026, FMVSS 108 on lighting, and FMVSS 135 on brakes, whose 26 June 2026 NPRM proposes removing the requirement that service brakes be foot-operated, with comments closing 27 July 2026. The GTR adopted at UNECE gives self-certification a common international basis.
What is blocking
The absence of a federal statute keeps every deployment exposed to state politics, and the exemption cap keeps purpose-built vehicles like Zoox's rationed. AV STEP, the voluntary oversight programme proposed in January 2025, was formally withdrawn on 26 June 2026, leaving transparency obligations thinner than the industry's critics wanted.
Who decides
The Department for Transport under the Automated Vehicles Act 2024, with the Vehicle Certification Agency determining which vehicles qualify, and local transport authorities holding a consent power that gives Transport for London an effective veto in the obvious first market.
The current instrument
The Act itself, with full implementation planned for the second half of 2027, and one part accelerated: the Automated Passenger Services permitting scheme, brought forward to spring 2026 via SI 2026/439, operationalising Part 5 of the Act.
What is permitted today
Applications opened 22 May 2026 for taxi, bus and private-hire-style self-driving services in England, Wales and Scotland, with or without a safety driver, and first paying journeys are expected later in 2026. It is the first time UK legislation has permitted passenger-carrying commercial autonomous services to the public. Wayve, Uber and Waymo have all publicly committed to participate, and Baidu and Uber plan a separate London pilot with Apollo Go RT6 vehicles.
What changes next
An Automated Passenger Services Accessibility Advisory Panel is due in Q3 to Q4 2026, along with draft regulations covering licensing of no-user-in-charge operators and automated self-driving entities. The SMMT sizes the UK automated passenger services market at £3.7B annually by 2040.
What is blocking
Nothing statutory before 2027 except capacity: the permitting scheme is new, the certification pipeline is new, and local consent adds a political layer per city.
Who decides
The European Commission through type approval, which is the structural difference from the US: vehicles must pass government testing before sale rather than being self-certified. National authorities grant the approvals; the framework incorporates UNECE regulations including UN R157 on Automated Lane Keeping.
The current instrument
Regulation 2022/1426 provides the two-step EU approval process for fully automated vehicles, covering both the vehicle and its operating area, now sitting beneath the June 2026 UNECE framework. The EU AI Act adds cross-cutting documentation duties for AI-driven vehicle systems.
What is permitted today
Two things are true at once, and the distinction matters. No Level 4 type approvals had been granted in Europe as of mid-2026: Germany has a legal framework for L4 driverless operation under its amended Road Traffic Act, and Level 3 is permitted where a driver can retake control, but the vehicle approvals themselves have not been issued. Separately, operating permission has begun to appear. In May 2026 Estonia authorised Berlin-based Bliq to run vehicles on public roads with no driver behind the wheel, under remote supervision, reported as the first such approval in an EU member state. Switzerland, outside the EU, has issued two L4 permits, both to Chinese operators. Permission to operate a service and type approval of a vehicle are different instruments, and Europe is moving faster on the first.
What changes next
The Commission has planned targeted type-approval changes to enable unlimited series production of automated vehicles, including regulatory sandboxes and automated driving corridors starting in 2026. The UNECE regulation gives those changes their technical backbone.
What is blocking
The type-approval culture itself: rigorous, slow, and until June 2026 without an agreed international standard for driverless systems to be approved against.

Who decides
MIIT with the Ministry of Transport and public security organs, through national standards and city-level pilot permissions. The most permissive regime for deployment, and the most willing to pause it when incidents occur.
The current instrument
City pilot permits for robotaxis; December 2025's first Level 3 permits for conventional passenger cars, to Changan's Deepal SL03 and BAIC's Arcfox Alpha S, with the Changan approval limited to congested single-lane operation up to 50 km/h on named Chongqing sections; and a draft mandatory national standard, released for comment 12 February 2026 and proposed for 1 July 2027, requiring L3 systems to execute a minimal risk manoeuvre unaided, a takeover window of no less than 10 seconds, and an aircraft-style DSSAD data recorder.
What is permitted today
Commercial driverless robotaxi service at scale across 27-plus cities cumulatively, led by Apollo Go, Pony.ai and WeRide, with fares charged. After the 31 March Wuhan outage, new permits froze nationwide from 29 April 2026; Bloomberg reported on 23 July 2026 that issuance is resuming gradually in selected cities after an industry-wide safety review, which I carry as reported rather than confirmed.
What changes next
The mandatory standard's 1 July 2027 date, with a 13-month transition after which non-compliant systems may not be produced, imported or sold. L2-and-above installation already reached 87.4 percent of new energy passenger vehicles in early 2026, so the standard will govern most new cars sold.
What is blocking
Regulatory confidence: the freeze demonstrated that the industry's permission to expand depends on avoiding another fleet-wide failure. April 2025's restriction on marketing terms such as "autonomous driving" for L2 systems applies a comparable approach to how these systems are described.
Who decides
National and emirate transport authorities, led by Abu Dhabi's Integrated Transport Centre, with Dubai's RTA and Saudi authorities following. Decisions come fast and at national level, which is the region's structural advantage.
The current instrument
Direct commercial permits: the ITC issued some of the emirate's first driverless commercial licences to Apollo Go with AutoGo and to WeRide with Uber, including WeRide's city-level fully driverless permit of November 2025.
What is permitted today
Paid driverless service in Abu Dhabi covering about 70 percent of core areas as of February 2026, Apollo Go operating in Dubai from February 2026, and the region's first fully driverless commercial operation running since November 2025 on Yas Island.
What changes next
Scale: WeRide and Uber committed in February 2026 to at least 1,200 robotaxis across Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Riyadh as soon as 2027.
What is blocking
The risk nobody models in a spreadsheet: WeRide suspended its Dubai fleet in March 2026 as the US and Israel conflict with Iran escalated, while Abu Dhabi and Riyadh continued. The same centralised authority that permits quickly can also pause quickly.
Switzerland
The quiet surprise of the map: two separate Level 4 permits issued to Chinese operators, WeRide's Furttal robotaxi permit of November 2025 covering 110 km of routes at up to 80 km/h, and Baidu's AmiGo special permit of 12 June 2026 for an 80 km² area with PostBus. A type-approval jurisdiction has issued more driverless permits than any EU member state to date.
Estonia
Authorised Bliq in May 2026 to operate on public roads with no driver behind the wheel, under remote supervision, reported as the first approval of its kind in an EU member state. The company runs around a dozen driverless vehicles in Tallinn.
Singapore
Deliberate and incremental: LTA approval for WeRide and Grab fleet-wide testing in Punggol from November 2025, consistent with a decade of controlled trials.
Japan
Type-approval market inside the UNECE system; Waymo is preparing Tokyo with Nihon Kotsu and GO, and the Wayve, Nissan and Uber pilot targets late 2026, each with safety operators first.
South Korea
Apollo Go launched in the Seoul metropolitan area in February 2026, the first large foreign robotaxi deployment in the country.
Everywhere else
Watching, drafting, and now holding a UNECE framework to draft against. The interesting question for 2027 is which mid-sized regulator moves first with a national approval under the new regulation.
