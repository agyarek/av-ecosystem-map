Tesla

Austin, US · Tesla, Inc. · robotaxi service since June 2025

Tesla is running the industry's largest architectural experiment: cameras only, no prior map dependence, one end-to-end network trained by millions of customer cars. The service is operating commercially; the open question is how quickly vehicle numbers follow the mapped service area.

How the driving system works

Cameras only, no lidar, no HD-map dependence, one neural network from pixels to controls. The strategic case is cost and generality: if it works, every Tesla built since the current hardware generation is a potential robotaxi, and the fleet's daily driving becomes training data no competitor can buy. The engineering case is still being made, and Tesla itself has tied scale to the FSD v15 rewrite, which grows the driving model from roughly 1B to about 10B parameters and is targeted for late 2026 or early 2027. Musk's argument is that deploying unsupervised at scale before that rewrite makes no sense.

One hardware constraint affects a large share of the existing fleet: Hardware 3 vehicles, a substantial share of the global fleet, lack the memory bandwidth for unsupervised operation and cannot be upgraded without replacing both the compute unit and the cameras. In practice this narrows the near-term robotaxi fleet to more recent vehicles.

The vehicle it drives

Today's service runs on production Model Ys, which is central to the strategy: no retrofit and no separate platform, just vehicles from the existing line. The purpose-built Cybercab remains the forward promise, and its production economics are a 2027 question. Morgan Stanley's December 2025 forecast of 1,000 Tesla robotaxis operating by the end of 2026 is the reference external estimate against which the current count of roughly 21 can be judged.

Funding

There is no robotaxi funding round to report because the programme is funded from Tesla's balance sheet and priced inside Tesla's stock. The economics argument is the interesting part: roughly $8 of revenue for a 5-mile ride against Waymo's $15 to 17, on the claim that a camera-only car with no sensor kit and no mapping bill costs less to run. Whether the lower cost is real is partly testable now through Austin's losses and partly a Cybercab production question. The fare history above, including a tripled base fare, suggests the first answer arrived and was not the hoped-for one.

Who it works with

Tesla is the least partnered operator on this map by design: own cars, own chips since Hardware 4, own app, own charging. The mapped relationships below are correspondingly few, which is itself the datum.

The biggest obstacle

What Tesla has not solved

The service area and the fleet are growing at different rates. Austin's geofence has covered roughly 245 square miles since 3 June 2026, while the active unsupervised fleet there moved from about 25 vehicles in late April to about 17 by 21 July, per community fleet tracking. Wide coverage at low vehicle density lengthens wait times, and density is currently gated on the FSD v15 rewrite and on a hardware generation that cannot be upgraded in place. The strategy is coherent; the question is when the software milestone arrives to unlock it.

What happens next

  • Whether FSD v15 ships to the robotaxi fleet in the stated late-2026 to early-2027 window, and whether the unsupervised count moves from tens toward Morgan Stanley's 1,000. Both are countable from filings and fleet tracking.
  • Whether the Nevada application converts into service, which would be the first expansion beyond Texas's permissive regime into a new state framework. Filed 5 June 2026 for up to 5,000 vehicles.
  • Whether Bay Area operation drops its safety drivers, which requires a California driverless deployment permit Tesla does not hold. California's DMV process is public; watch the register.
