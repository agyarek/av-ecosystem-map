Unit economics: utilisation is the lever the whole business turns on

Strip the branding away and every operator on this map runs the same machine: vehicles that cost $50,000 to $150,000 with their autonomy kit, driven as many paid miles per day as demand allows, against costs that are mostly fixed per mile. The lever that makes any of it plausible is utilisation. A private car works about an hour a day; a robotaxi can work twenty. That twentyfold difference is what spreads the hardware bill thin enough to compete with a human driver who brings their own car to work.

The one number that matters is fully-loaded cost per revenue mile. Third-party analysis puts Waymo's 2025 figure near $1.98. Every bull case in the sector reduces to the same claim: that number halves. At $1.98 against a roughly $16 fare over eight miles, a vehicle earns back very little. At $0.99, contribution rises to roughly $70,000 per vehicle per year and a $90,000 purpose-built robotaxi pays for itself in about sixteen months.

Provenance warning, before you quote anything. No operator publishes fully-loaded cost per mile. The $1.98 figure is a third-party analyst estimate circulating across several publications, and at least one trail leads back to a Reddit analysis. That is the state of public knowledge in this industry, which is exactly why the calculator below exists: substitute your own assumptions and stop depending on anyone's estimate, including mine.

The published cost breakdown, and why it is a target

Published breakdowns for a mature operation land in the $0.30 to $0.50 per mile range: vehicle depreciation at $0.08 to $0.12 amortised over 500,000-plus miles, energy $0.03 to $0.05, maintenance $0.03 to $0.05, insurance $0.05 to $0.08 declining as safety data accumulates, remote monitoring $0.05 to $0.10 declining with autonomy quality, and technology and compute $0.03 to $0.05. Treat that as a destination rather than a description. Nobody operates there today, and the gap between it and the $1.98 estimate is the industry's entire remaining homework.

Four operators, four different bets

Waymo carries the reference cost structure: the most vehicles, the most cities, and a $126B February 2026 valuation against roughly $355M of annualised revenue, which is about 355 times revenue. The sixth-generation hardware at a reported sub-$20,000 kit cost is its answer to the arithmetic. Tesla prices a five-mile ride near $8 against Waymo's $15 to 17, betting that cameras-only hardware and no mapping bill halve the cost line; the claim is partly testable now through Austin's losses and partly a 2027 question about Cybercab production. That its base fare tripled in March 2026 suggests the first data arrived and argued back.

Baidu has the cheaper vehicle and the denser anchor city, and a reasonable inference is that Apollo Go reaches company-level profitability before Waymo does. Its unit breakeven claims in Wuhan, like Pony.ai's in Shenzhen and Guangzhou, are vehicle-level in single cities, and the distinction between that and company profitability should stay visible whenever either is quoted.

Aurora offers the cleanest comparison in the industry because its benchmark is a freight rate rather than a subsidised fare. A 2026 target of 200-plus driverless trucks against an $80M revenue run rate implies about $400,000 per truck per year, against $300,000 to $450,000 for a conventional Class 8 truck with a driver, where removing the driver saves roughly $70,000 to $90,000 a year. The headroom is thinner than the robotaxi story suggests, which is why Aurora sells reliability and insurance economics as hard as it sells the driver.

Where the arithmetic actually stands

Where the arithmetic actually stands

Uber's CFO said plainly, on the record, in August 2025: autonomous vehicles today are not profitable. Alphabet's Other Bets, which contains Waymo, lost $2.1B in Q1 2026. Every published breakeven claim in the industry is per-vehicle in a single city. The bull case requires the cost line to halve, and the evidence that it will is a hardware roadmap, not an income statement. That is not a dismissal; it is a timestamp.
