■ WHAT HAS GONE WRONG

The incident and
recall record

A factual record of what has gone wrong in autonomous vehicle operations: the incidents, the software recalls, and the measurement problems that make published safety comparisons difficult to evaluate. Presented as an operations review rather than as advocacy in either direction.

01The measurement problem, first

Waymo's own comparative safety claim circulates in at least three forms across 2026 sources: 13 times fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes than human drivers (June 2026), 90 percent fewer serious crashes (February 2026), and 91 percent fewer serious injury crashes (December 2025). These are not necessarily contradictory, since they may cover different denominators and periods. Published without their methodology, though, they are difficult to compare or verify. This site shows all three with dates, and notes that the underlying exposure data is not independently auditable.

Mileage figures disagree the same way: 170M-plus autonomous miles per a June 2026 Waymo statement, against 220M-plus rider-only miles through March 2026 in earlier compilations. Rider-only miles and total autonomous miles are different denominators, and a comparison that does not define its denominator first cannot be evaluated. The general rule this page applies: define terms, date figures, and prefer filings over press.

02The incident register

Chronological, each entry carrying what happened and what the regulator did. The record shows a shift over time in how the industry responds: from a fatality that ended a programme, to software recalls that update fleets within weeks.

Mar 2018Uber ATG A test vehicle struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona, the industry's first pedestrian fatality. Programme-ending. Uber exited AV development; the division sold to Aurora in 2020, and Uber has not built a driver since.
Oct 2023Cruise A robotaxi struck a pedestrian thrown into its path by another car, then dragged her while attempting to pull over in San Francisco. Licence-ending. California DMV revoked the permit; Cruise suspended driverless operations nationwide and filed an ADS recall that November.
Dec 2024Cruise / GM GM shut the robotaxi business entirely. Folded into GM's personal-vehicle ADAS group in February 2025. The capital stopped before the regulator had to.
Jan 2025NHTSA on Cruise Closed its preliminary investigation after analysing 2,759 collision reports including 1,113 pedestrian conflicts, finding five pedestrian collisions with three injuries and avoiding action taken in each. Closed without further action, a finding that is less widely cited than the incident itself.
Dec 2025Waymo A San Francisco power outage killed traffic signals; robotaxis halted in traffic and caused gridlock. Political. A 2 March 2026 city hearing; SF Emergency Management said first responders had become default roadside assistance and called it untenable; Waymo shipped a software update.
Jan 2026Waymo A robotaxi struck a child near a school in Santa Monica, with minor injuries. Escalating. NHTSA and NTSB both opened investigations into behaviour around school buses.
31 Mar 2026Baidu Apollo Go More than 100 robotaxis stopped simultaneously across Wuhan after a cloud-linked failure, stranding passengers up to two hours; SOS buttons returned unavailability messages and support calls disconnected. Industry-freezing. A joint MIIT, MoT and PSB meeting on 14 April; Wuhan operations suspended; nationwide permit freeze from 29 April, reported easing only in late July.
Apr 2025Xiaomi A fatal SU7 crash in driver-assist mode, in the adjacent consumer-ADAS world. Language-changing. MIIT banned misleading marketing terms including "autonomous driving" and "hands-free driving" for L2 systems.

03Recalls: a genuinely new regulatory object

A software recall does not summon cars to a workshop; it patches a fleet over the air and files the paperwork that makes the defect public. Waymo has now filed six, and the register reads like an engineering syllabus: June 2024, 672 vehicles after a collision with a utility pole in a Phoenix alley. May 2025, 1,212 vehicles for minor collisions with gates and chains. December 2025, 3,000-plus vehicles for illegally passing stopped school buses in Austin.

Then 2026 turned the pattern serious. On 12 May, approximately 3,800 vehicles for a defect allowing them to drive onto flooded roadways, affecting fifth and sixth-generation systems, with operating limits imposed during extreme weather. On 18 June, 3,871 vehicles after 13 incidents of robotaxis entering closed freeway construction zones, six near Phoenix in April and seven in the Bay Area in May; the NHTSA filing states vehicles may enter and drive at posted speed by inappropriately prioritising avoidance of other hazards or failing to recognise the closure, covering fifth-generation systems manufactured May 2022 to May 2026. Waymo restricted freeway operations from 19 May, and as of 13 June the remedy was still under development.

Waymo began offering freeway rides in November 2025 and had them suspended within seven months. Both halves of that sequence matter: a service was withdrawn within months of launch, and a defect affecting nearly 4,000 vehicles was contained by a software restriction within days, which has no equivalent in a human-driven fleet.

04The interesting category: fleet-wide failure

The question the industry has not answered

The Wuhan outage and the San Francisco power cut are qualitatively different from every per-vehicle defect above, because they were simultaneous and fleet-wide, and because each turned a private product into a public-safety problem within minutes. Industry engineers attributed the Wuhan stall to a safety self-check mechanism, which is the same shape as Waymo's dark-signal behaviour: each vehicle failed safe, and the fleet failed the city. Individually correct, collectively catastrophic. Robotaxis are now an emergency-services dependency, and no operator has published what its fleet does when the cloud, the grid or the network goes away.

05What nobody publishes

Comparable per-mile exposure data across operators. Every safety claim on this page is self-reported against a denominator its author chose. Until operators publish audited exposure data on a common definition, the honest summary of AV safety is: the per-vehicle record is encouraging, the fleet-level record contains two significant failures, and the comparison to human driving currently rests on figures the operators compile themselves.
