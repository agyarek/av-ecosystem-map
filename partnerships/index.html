The most consequential player operates no vehicles

The most consequential strategic behaviour on this map belongs to a company that operates no autonomous vehicles. Uber runs more than twenty AV partnerships across ride-hail, delivery and freight, and has moved from signing partners to financing them: $500M into Lucid for an 11.5 percent stake with at least 35,000 Nuro-powered vehicles committed, up to $250M into Waabi tied to 25,000 robotaxis, up to $375M into Avride with Nebius, milestone capital into Wayve, participation in Nuro's Series E, and up to $1.25B into Rivian by 2031 for 10,000 autonomous R2 robotaxis in San Francisco and Miami from 2028, expanding toward 50,000 vehicles by 2030. In this site's funding timeline Uber appears as an investor in seven separate events, which is the capital-for-supply thesis written in term sheets.

The strategy has an honest counterweight, spoken by Uber's own CFO on the 6 August 2025 earnings call: autonomous vehicles today are not profitable. AVs remain about 0.1 percent of rideshare trips globally, and Uber's AV partnerships were generating an annualised rate of about 1.5M trips as of late 2025 against a platform measured in tens of billions. Uber is not betting that autonomy is profitable now. It is paying to own the front door for whenever it becomes so, while its AV Labs division, launched January 2026, sends sensor-equipped cars into cities to collect the data its partners need, and its Hertz and Oro Mobility deal of 30 April 2026 outsources the depots for the Lucid fleet. Having exited AV development after the 2018 fatality and sold the division to Aurora in 2020, Uber is explicit that it is never building the driver again. It intends to own everything around the driver instead.

Lyft plays the same game with less capital and one genuinely clever move: Waymo rides in Nashville run on Lyft, and Lyft's Flexdrive subsidiary services and manages that Waymo fleet, which means Lyft is selling fleet operations to the strongest competitor its core business has. May Mobility runs on Lyft in Atlanta, Mobileye and Marubeni are planned for Dallas, and an August 2025 agreement brings Baidu Apollo Go to Europe from 2026, beginning with Germany and the UK. Via supplies the transit software layer for AV shuttles across hundreds of cities. Grab tests with WeRide in Singapore, FREE NOW anchors European ride-hail distribution, and the Chinese aggregators, DiDi and CaoCao among them, hold the same position domestically, with DiDi Autonomous Driving additionally building its own driver on a $281M October 2025 raise.

Moved out of the medallion, these platforms got more room, not less. The centre of the wall chart now holds only organisations whose driver a passenger can meet, and the demand layer gets what it actually is: the industry's distribution, its emerging financier, and the party most likely to own the customer whoever wins the driving.

Waymo's vehicle chain runs four deep: Jaguar Land Rover for the I-PACE, Zeekr for the purpose-built Ojai, Hyundai for IONIQ 5s with 50,000 units lined up, Magna co-operating the Mesa integration plant, and a preliminary Toyota alliance beyond them. The lidar market split in two: Hesai says lidar cost has fallen 99.5 percent in eight years and reports 4M ATX orders, RoboSense shipped 719,200 units in the first half of 2026, up 169.6 percent, while Luminar filed Chapter 11 on 15 December 2025 and Velodyne and Quanergy were already gone. Western lidar consolidated; Chinese lidar industrialised.
