■ PASSENGER AUTONOMY

The autonomous drivers a
passenger can actually meet

These are the organisations whose driver carries members of the public today or is verifiably about to. Platforms that sell the ride live on Partnerships; suppliers that build the parts live on the wall chart. Here, only the drivers.

What puts a company on this page. One test: its driver — the software doing the driving — carries members of the public today, or is verifiably about to. The test is about the driver, not the fleet, so it takes in companies that own no vehicles and leaves out companies that own many but buy their driving in. Whoever meets it belongs here, and the list grows when the industry does. The boundary cases are worked through below.

The choices that separate them

Every operator answers the same five questions differently: how the car sees, who builds the vehicle, where demand comes from, whether anyone pays yet, and whose money is at risk. Those answers, side by side, are most of what you need to know.

Company by company

WA

Waymo

The only fully driverless paid service at scale: about 500k rides a week across 11 US metros.

The hard part: six recalls, a suspended freeway service, and the arithmetic behind its own million-ride target.
BA

Baidu Apollo Go

Largest robotaxi operation on earth by cumulative rides, and the most international.

The hard part: one cloud outage stopped a city's fleet and froze a national industry for three months.
TE

Tesla

Largest vision-only fleet feeding one training loop, with a metro-wide map.

The hard part: the map covers 245 square miles; the unsupervised fleet is about 17 cars.
ZO

Zoox

First purpose-built robotaxi with no controls to carry public riders.

The hard part: it cannot charge a fare anywhere until a federal exemption lands.
PA

Pony.ai

First operator to claim city-level unit-economics breakeven, in Shenzhen and Guangzhou.

The hard part: breakeven in two cities is not breakeven as a company.
WE

WeRide

Licensed in eight countries, the broadest regulatory footprint of any operator.

The hard part: geopolitics suspended its Dubai fleet in a week.
WA

Wayve

Map-light end-to-end driving licensed to OEMs and fleets rather than operated alone.

The hard part: it has not yet run a commercial driverless service anywhere.
NU

Nuro

Pivoted from delivery pods to licensing its driver; now powering Uber's Lucid fleet.

The hard part: its scale depends entirely on partners executing.
MM

May Mobility

Constrained-domain transit and campus autonomy, 3.5M+ rides, live on Lyft.

The hard part: small domains may not generalise to open urban demand.

Motional

Hyundai can factory-integrate its robotaxi, and Uber remains its route to market.

The hard part: paused, reset, and relaunching with safety operators aboard.

What qualifies a company for this list

The octagon at the centre of the wall chart holds organisations whose driving software a member of the public can meet, as distinct from the platforms that sell the ride and the suppliers that build the parts. A passenger booking through Uber is carried by a driver or, increasingly, by a Waymo vehicle that Uber has matched to the trip.

That definition excludes Uber, Lyft and Via, whose strategic position is covered in depth on Partnerships, where there is room to treat it properly. It includes Wayve and Nuro, who run almost nothing themselves but whose drivers will carry the public in other people's fleets. And it includes Baidu Apollo Go, whose absence from the centre of any global AV map would be the first thing an informed reader noticed.

DiDi Autonomous Driving does not meet the test: credible Level 4 work and a $281M raise in October 2025, but its driverless service remains inside a ride-hail business I treat as demand. The boundary is a judgement call, and it is stated here so readers can apply their own.
