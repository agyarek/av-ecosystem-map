■ CAN I BUY ONE?

The part that went
backwards

While robotaxi fleets scaled through 2026, the car you can buy became less autonomous. Consumer Level 3 availability contracted, on purpose, for reasons that are more commercial than technical, and that inversion is the story of this page.

01What was withdrawn, and why

Mercedes-Benz Drive Pilot was the only Level 3 system certified for US roads, limited to California and Nevada at 40 mph or below on the S-Class and EQS, sold by subscription. In early 2026 Mercedes decided to suspend its rollout, citing stringent usage conditions and high cost against limited practical value, and dropped it from new models from the 2026 S-Class, substituting a Level 2++ system. Existing 2023 to 2025 cars keep what they bought. BMW confirmed in March 2026 that the facelifted 7 Series drops the Personal Pilot L3 option, on a take rate below 5 percent, pivoting to a cheaper, broader L2+ system. Honda's Sensing Elite, on Japanese roads in the Legend since 2021, has been discontinued.

The one-line explanation, and it is a legal line rather than a technical one: Level 3 moves liability from the driver to the manufacturer for the duration of the engagement. Carrying that liability requires conservative operating conditions, and those conditions narrow the feature enough that buyers have been reluctant to pay for it. No volume manufacturer has yet reported a take rate that justifies the exposure.

02What you can actually buy today

Level 2, in increasingly capable forms: Tesla FSD (Supervised), GM Super Cruise across roughly 750,000 miles of mapped road, Ford BlueCruise, BMW Highway Assistant, Nissan ProPILOT 2.0 and Rivian's Universal Hands-Free. All four of the hands-free highway systems are restricted to divided highways and will not engage on city streets or at intersections, lights or stop signs. The driver remains legally responsible at every moment, which is the entire definition.

Into the gap between L2's legal reality and L3's withdrawn promise, marketing has inserted "Level 2++": systems that extend hands-off operation toward city streets and may stop at lights and signs while keeping every gram of liability on the driver. It is a marketing category rather than an SAE level, and it currently does much of the persuasive work in autonomy advertising, so it is worth recognising when you encounter it.

03What China did differently

China went the other way, deliberately. In December 2025 MIIT issued the country's first Level 3 permits for conventional passenger cars, to Changan's Deepal SL03 and BAIC's Arcfox Alpha S, with Li Auto holding Beijing road-test permits, Huawei's HIMA testing in Shenzhen, and BYD, Nio, FAW and SAIC in the approval pipeline. Then in February 2026 it published a draft mandatory standard for 1 July 2027 that writes the safety case into national law: a takeover window of no less than ten seconds, a minimal-risk manoeuvre the system must execute unaided, and a flight-recorder-style DSSAD data logger.

The commercial logic is equally explicit: research cited around MIIT reporting found nearly 70 percent of middle-income Chinese consumers aged 30 to 45 willing to pay an extra RMB 30,000 to 50,000 for advanced intelligent driving, and L2-and-above installation reached 87.4 percent of new energy passenger vehicles in early 2026. Huawei's automotive unit estimated highway L3 availability in 2026, with Avatr first to adopt. Western manufacturers have found the liability difficult to price at current take rates. Chinese manufacturers are addressing a market where customers already pay for advanced driver assistance, alongside a regulator moving to standardise it.

05The honest summary

If you walked into a showroom today

In the US and Europe, what is available to buy today is driver assistance, increasingly capable but still requiring supervision. The only certified consumer L3 system in America is no longer offered on new cars. In China you can buy the first permitted L3 sedans under a regime that is writing the rulebook as it scales. Meanwhile the fully driverless vehicles on public roads belong to fleets, because fleets can amortise the hardware, employ the safety engineers and carry the liability that consumer pricing cannot. The difference between the robotaxi and the private autonomous car is less a matter of software than of who can carry the cost and the liability.
